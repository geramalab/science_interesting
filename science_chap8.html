⏱️ Chapter 8: Rate of Reaction

Understand slow and fast chemical reactions, factors affecting the rate of reaction, and applications in daily life.

⚡ 8.1 Meaning of Rate of Reaction

A measure of how fast or slow a chemical reaction takes place.

  • Fast Reactions: Occur in a very short time (e.g., burning of magnesium ribbon, fireworks explosion, neutralization).
  • Slow Reactions: Take a long time to complete (e.g., rusting of iron, fermentation, photosynthesis).
  • Measuring Rate of Reaction: Involves observing the decrease in reactants or increase in products over time (e.g., volume of gas released or change in mass).

🌡️ 8.2 Factors Affecting Rate of Reaction

Four main factors influence how fast chemical reactions proceed:

  • Temperature of Reactants: Higher temperature increases reaction rate because reactant particles gain kinetic energy and collide more frequently.
  • Concentration / Pressure: Higher concentration or pressure increases particle crowding, leading to more frequent collisions.
  • Particle Size (Total Surface Area): Smaller particle sizes (e.g., powdered calcium carbonate vs large lumps) provide a larger surface area, speeding up the reaction.
  • Catalyst: A substance that changes the speed of a reaction without being chemically changed at the end of the reaction (e.g., manganese dioxide in hydrogen peroxide decomposition).

🏭 8.3 Applications in Daily Life and Industry

Controlling reaction rates is vital in food preservation, cooking, and manufacturing.

  • In Daily Life:
    - Storing food in a refrigerator slows down bacteria growth (low temperature).
    - Using a pressure cooker cooks food faster by increasing pressure and temperature.
  • In Industry:
    - The Haber Process uses high pressure, temperature, and an iron catalyst to manufacture ammonia efficiently.
    - Controlling coal dust explosions in mines and flour dust in factories.

💥 8.4 Application of Collision Theory

Explaining reaction rates through particle collisions.

  • For a reaction to occur, reactant particles must collide with adequate energy (activation energy) and correct orientation.
  • Effective collisions result in a chemical reaction. Increasing the frequency of effective collisions increases the rate of reaction.
